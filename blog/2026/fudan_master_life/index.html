Reflections on My Three Years at Fudan

  • Academics: 1/5
  • Sports: 4.5/5

After hesitating for a long time, I finally decided to write down a summary of my time here.

The Research Dilemma

During my second year, my advisor tasked me with exploring a dynamic system problem over finite fields:

(Conjecture) For a polynomial

\[f = a_dx^d + a_{d-1}x^{d-1} + \dots + a_0 \in \mathbb{F}_{p}[X],\]

assume $p$ is a large prime satisfying $d \mid (p-1)$ and

\[f^{\circ i}(0) \neq f^{\circ j}(0) \quad\text{for } 0 \leq i,j \leq N,\]

where $N$ is an integer like $\lceil \frac{p}{20} \rceil$. Here, $f^{\circ i}$ denotes the $i$-fold composition \(\underbrace{f \circ f \dots \circ f}_{i \text{ times}}.\)

The target was to prove that \(\# f^{\circ N}(\mathbb{F}_p) \sim \frac{p}{(d-1)N}.\)

Note: The assumption $d \mid (p-1)$ and the final asymptotic formula were initial guesses, and my core task was to deduce the actual behavior of this dynamic system.

Although my advisor and I met regularly, and he suggested several potential proof strategies, I hit a complete standstill. By my third year, his disappointment became palpable. When I shared my plans to apply for PhD programs at institutions like UW–Madison and Stony Brook, he declined to write recommendation letters for me.

Looking back, he had been a supportive advisor. He provided valuable guidance and even supplemented my stipend by 1,000 RMB per month during the second semester of my second year. He likely wanted me to dedicate all my energy to research—especially after I mentioned taking on a weekly tutoring job to supplement my income. (Surviving as a master’s student on the base monthly stipend of 600 RMB was nearly impossible, even with an additional 900 RMB from a teaching assistantship).

He also invited me to academic conferences in Xi’an and Nanjing. Those trips turned out to be pivotal: I made new friends, but I also realized I was on the wrong path. I was particularly humbled by a fellow student from the ALGANT (Algebra, Geometry, and Number Theory) program who had graduated from Nanjing University in the same cohort; his depth of knowledge far exceeded mine. Additionally advanced research about arithematic geometry is quite out of my league, prompting me to pivot my PhD applications toward applied mathematics instead.

The TOEFL Battle

Simultaneously, the TOEFL exam turned into a marathon of its own.

I spent the entire summer between my second and third year preparing, aiming for a score above 95—the benchmark for most mathematics departments. My preparation was relentless: I reviewed a high-frequency vocabulary list of 4,391 words three full times, completed TPO 40 through 54, and practiced speaking English out loud every day (to the point where my neighbors knew I was preparing to study abroad).

Attempt 1 (Nanjing Road Test Center, Shanghai) Going in, I felt confident that a 30 in Reading and a 25 in Listening would comfortably push me past 95. However, the high-pressure environment got to me, and I ran into an unfamiliar topic in the listening section.

  • Reading: 26 Listening: 21 Speaking: 20 Writing: 22
  • Total: 89

Seeing the instant subscores right after the writing section was a heavy blow. An 89 meant I had to register again and shell out another $300.

Attempt 2 (Shanghai Open University) Located close to Fudan, this attempt was even more frustrating.

  • Reading: Improved slightly
  • Listening: Dropped below 20
  • Total: 89 (again)

This was a devastating period. Facing a stagnant test score alongside the reality that my advisor wouldn’t provide recommendations made me feel completely hopeless.

Attempt 3 (Xi’an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, Suzhou) I desperately needed more time to prepare, but application deadlines were looming. A test date after December 1st meant scores wouldn’t arrive until late December. To make matters worse, all test seats in Shanghai were sold out, forcing me to book an exam in Suzhou for December 20th.

I arrived in Suzhou on December 19th into brisk winter weather and checked into a hotel near the campus. On exam day, things finally clicked. Right after finishing the reading and listening sections, I felt 80% sure I would break 95. While my speaking performance remained consistently weak, I got lucky on the integrated writing section with a familiar topic on music education in schools.

Upon submitting, the instant screen read:

  • Reading: 29 Listening: 27

Because of the Christmas holidays, the official report was delayed, making for an anxious wait into the new year. When the final breakdown arrived (R29 / L27 / S20 / W27 — Total: 103), it was a tremendous relief, allowing me to finally move forward with my applications.
